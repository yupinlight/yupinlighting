Colour rendering describes how a light source makes objects look compared with daylight. It is measured on the colour rendering index, where 100 represents daylight and a low number means colours are dulled or shifted. For decorative lighting in hospitality and residential interiors, the difference between CRI 80 and CRI 92 is visible without instruments.

## What changes at 92+

At CRI 80, reds and skin tones lose saturation and wood finishes can look grey. At CRI 92+, the same room reads warmer and more natural, and materials such as marble, brass and fabric keep the colour the designer selected. This matters most where people see themselves and each other: bathrooms, dining rooms, hotel guest rooms and reception areas.

The effect is not just aesthetic. When a buyer compares a sample under a low-CRI source with the same sample in their showroom, they may conclude that the finish or the material is wrong. High CRI removes that ambiguity.

## Reading the specification

CRI is usually stated as a single number, for example 92+, measured as Ra. Ask for the test report rather than the label, and check that the value applies to the whole fixture, not only the LED chip. A diffuser that absorbs light also affects the delivered spectrum slightly, which is why delivered lumen and CRI should be quoted together.

Colour temperature and CRI are independent. A 3000K fixture can have poor or excellent rendering. Do not assume that a warm colour temperature implies a high CRI.

## Where to spend the extra cost

High CRI adds cost at the LED and driver level, so it is worth targeting. Specify 92+ for fixtures used at close range or in hospitality interiors: vanity lights, pendants over dining tables, bedside reading lights and reception lighting. For service corridors, plant rooms and exterior facades, CRI 80 is usually adequate and the saving is real.

If a project mixes both, keep the colour temperature consistent across the two specifications, otherwise the difference in rendering becomes visible from one area to the next.
